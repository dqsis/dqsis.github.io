You Cannot Step Into the Same River Twice

You cannot step into the same river twice, because other waters are always flowing toward you. The river keeps its name. The water never repeats.

Heraclitus, c. 500 BCE — preserved as a fragment through Plato's Cratylus. I was reminded of it by DHH (David Heinemeier Hansson), on his appearance on the Lex Fridman Podcast.
Note

I keep running into this one in places that have nothing to do with philosophy.

Every year I go back to my country of origin for the holidays. My parents, relatives, old friends still carry an image of who I was when I left. But that person does not exist anymore. Years abroad, different work, different daily pressures, they all reshaped me into someone they are not fully familiar with. And it runs both ways. They have changed too, in ways I only half see because I am not there for the slow accumulation of it. We meet expecting the old river. We get new water.

Same thing watching my daughters grow. One year they are children. A few years later they are teenagers with their own opinions, their own friction points, their own version of the world. I cannot parent the teenager the way I parented the child, even though she carries the same name and the same photographs from before.

Software has its own version of this. Customer requirements are never the fixed thing a contract or a spec implies. What a product needed to do a month ago is not what it needs to do now. Cybersecurity is a clean example. Requirements that barely existed a few months back now show up mid-sprint, forcing teams to rework priorities around demands that were not there when the roadmap was first drawn. Nobody planned poorly. The regulatory water just kept moving.

The lesson is not to plan harder so the river holds still. It won't. Since requirements change constantly, the only sane response is to accept change itself as the one constant and build the capacity to adjust to it as it comes, rather than treating the original plan as sacred. This is exactly what the Agile Manifesto and its authors have been advocating since 2001: "responding to change over following a plan." Two decades on, teams still relearn it the hard way, project by project, because the instinct to defend the original spec is stronger than the evidence that the water has already moved.

The mistake, in families and in product development, is treating the name as if it guarantees the substance. It does not. The river is still called a river. The product is still called the same product. But what is actually flowing through it changes constantly, and pretending otherwise is how you end up planning for a version of the world that already left.
