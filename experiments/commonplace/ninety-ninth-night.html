The Ninety-Ninth Night

A mandarin fell in love with a courtesan. She told him she would be his if he waited for her, sitting on a stool in her garden, for one hundred nights in a row. He agreed, and night after night he sat beneath her window, patient and unmoving. On the ninety-ninth night, with only one night left, he suddenly rose, tucked his stool under his arm, and walked away.

Retold by Roland Barthes in A Lover’s Discourse: Fragments (1977), in the fragment “Waiting.” I first met it in Έρωτας και άλλες πολεμικές τέχνες.
Note

That is the whole story. No reason is given for why he leaves — and the silence is the point.

It is usually read as a meditation on desire and waiting. One reading says his desire died precisely because it was about to be satisfied: the anticipation was the love, and possession would only have ended it. Another reads pride into it — a man who will not be tested to the very last inch, and who reclaims himself by refusing the final step. A third finds simple exhaustion: the limit of any patience, which can turn, without warning, into refusal. Barthes uses it to show how the lover’s waiting is “unbearable” — how devotion and abandonment sit closer together than we like to think.

What I find striking is where the story comes from. Barthes borrowed it from the Japanese legend of the poet Ono no Komachi and Captain Fukakusa no Shōshō — “The Hundred Nights.” In the original, the suitor does not walk away on the last night. He dies on it. Barthes’ quiet edit — turning a death into a decision — is the whole difference between tragedy and freedom. The captain is defeated by the hundredth night; the mandarin defeats it.
