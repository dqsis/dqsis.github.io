Dissatisfaction Is an Acquired Taste

Hedonic adaptation — a fancy term that simply means what once felt extraordinary becomes ordinary over time. Last year’s raise is now the paycheck you expect every two weeks. And yesterday’s upgrade is now today’s obsolescence.

The thing we obtain isn’t the flashy car or the giant house or the impressive job title. What we really acquire is a taste for those things — a synthetic thirst that now needs repeated quenching. So in a real way, dissatisfaction is an acquired taste.

This is why the path of improvement eventually leads to deficiency. The moment we decide water must taste better, we have declared that water itself is insufficient.

The Minimalists (Joshua Fields Millburn & Ryan Nicodemus). See also the hedonic treadmill.
Note

What stuck with me is the reversal. I always thought I was chasing the thing — the camera, the raise, the better apartment. But what I really pick up is the wanting. The thing wears off fast; the taste for it stays. That’s the trap of an inflated lifestyle: earn a bit more and the new amount just becomes normal, so the raise that made me happy in month one is simply the salary I expect by month three.

So the real question about the mirrorless camera isn’t can I afford it, or even would it take better photos. It’s: am I buying a tool, or am I buying a new want that I’ll have to feed again in a couple of months? If the happiness only lasts a few weeks, I’m not really buying an object — I’m signing up for the next craving.

“The moment we decide water must taste better, we have declared that water itself is insufficient.” The point isn’t to give everything up. It’s to learn that the water is already enough.
